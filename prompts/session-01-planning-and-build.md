# Competitions and Take-Homes

Target first place, not "good." Everything below exists because skipping it lost a
real competition. Only the non-obvious rules are here; ordinary good engineering is
assumed.

Italicised notes are worked examples from past competitions, kept because a rule
with a scar attached gets followed. They illustrate the rule; they do not limit it
to that domain. Translate each one into the domain at hand.

## Create the tracker first

Before planning, create `COMPETITION.md` at the project root (template at the
bottom). Update it in the same turn anything in it changes. Rules reload every
session; accumulated state does not, and state is what evaporates across a long
build — what the field count actually was, which prize buckets are confirmed,
which claims already have proof.

**Then wire it in, in the same turn.** Append this to the project's own
`CLAUDE.md`, creating that file if it does not exist:

```markdown
## Competition
This project is a judged submission. Read `COMPETITION.md` before doing anything
else — it holds the rubric, the field count, the prize buckets, the claims and
their proofs, and the freeze checklist. Keep it current.
```

Do this at kickoff and do not skip it. Project `CLAUDE.md` is loaded automatically
every session; the skill is not guaranteed to be. Without that line, the tracker is
guarded by the same memory it exists to replace, and a later session can rediscover
questions this one already answered — or skip them because they feel handled.

## First: what kind of competition is this?

Decide this before anything else and record it in the tracker. It changes which of
the rules below apply and where the hours go.

**Engineering-judged** (the default). Rigor and evidence decide it. Everything
below applies as written.

**Design-, UX- or consumer-judged.** When craft, delight or user experience appear
in the scoring criteria, polish *is* the product. "Rigor over polish" and
"submission surface has a ceiling" then apply only to pitch materials, never to the
artifact itself. Proof still matters — here it is a real person completing a real
task unaided.

**Leaderboard or metric-scored** (Kaggle-style, benchmark, CTF, speed run). No
judges, no narrative, no demo. Almost nothing below applies except real inputs,
honest validation, the freeze reserve, and not manufacturing work. The whole game
is the metric and a validation setup that doesn't lie to you.

**Live-pitch or stage-judged.** The presentation is the artifact. Rehearse it, time
it, prepare the three hostile questions, build the demo fallback.

**Take-home or hiring assignment.** No field to count, no sponsor, usually no video.
Carries over: scope-pays-for-proof, one proof per claim, showing limits, publishing
mistakes, the freeze reserve, precision. Add: satisfy every stated requirement
before anything optional, and review the repo as a skeptical senior engineer
comparing many candidates — the first file they open is the README, the second is
the tests.

---

## Before choosing the idea

**Read the actual rules.** Criteria, tracks, eligibility, deadlines, submission
requirements. Never plan from memory when the source is available.

**Establish judging mechanics before the idea.** Who judges, on what artifact
(repo, live demo, video only), and what winning requires logistically. This can
eliminate whole categories regardless of build quality.

**Enumerate every prize bucket** — tracks, side categories, bonus awards, feedback
surveys, update posts. List each in the tracker with an entered/not-entered status
and confirm each with Ashraf. *DataHub 2026: 17 badged winners, 10 of them from a
feedback survey that was flagged, never confirmed, never entered.*

**Study why past winners won.** Extract the reasons, don't copy the projects.

## Know the field, and act on it

**Lead every "how are my chances" answer with the base rate.** Entrant count, prize
count, percentage, then placement. Never substitute a comparison against three
known competitors — the field is the denominator, not the sample. If the count is
unpublished, say so and estimate.

**Count how many entries share your core mechanic**, from the full gallery or
candidate list. Then branch, and record the branch:

- 0–1 share it → proceed.
- 2–4 share it → the mechanic no longer differentiates. Name in writing which of
  precision, proof, or scope-of-action is the differentiator, and lead the demo
  with it.
- 5+ share it → commodity. Either find an axis nobody is on, or tell Ashraf plainly
  that placing now means being the best execution of a common idea.

**Assume the obvious application of the sponsor's headline feature is the most
crowded track, not the least.** *ML lineage was assessed as uncontested; five badged
winners shared it.*

A competitor surfacing in a community channel is not "one competitor" — it is proof
the idea is reachable. Go count.

## Finding the idea

Generate many and kill fast. Do not converge on the first plausible concept — that
is how a project ends up being the fourth entry doing the obvious thing.

**Start with the exclusion list.** Where there is a sponsor or set platform, write
down the three or four obvious applications of its headline feature; otherwise the
obvious readings of the brief. Those are what most of the field
will build. Do not pick from that list unless the plan is to be the best execution
of a common idea, chosen deliberately.

**Then generate against these shapes.** Every winner in a strong field fits one;
they are reliable generators, not a taxonomy to admire.

- **The silent failure.** Find the break where every existing check passes and
  nothing alarms. Not the outage — the wrong number nobody notices for three days.
  *A payment provider switches to cents, every schema validates, every job is green,
  and the revenue dashboard is wrong by 100x.* This shape won the grand prize and
  two categories.
- **Transplant a familiar tool.** "A stack trace for model decay." "git diff for
  business logic." A judge understands it before the sentence ends, and the name
  does the explaining.
- **Invert the ranking axis.** Everyone ranks by severity; rank by how much harm
  actually reaches someone. Everyone counts downstream nodes; count downstream
  *use*. The inversion is the whole idea.
- **Subtraction.** The value is removing noise, not finding more. *"Most alerts
  about your pipelines don't matter. This tells you which five out of a thousand
  do."*
- **Invert the sponsor's premise.** *The platform gives agents context; make context
  decide what agents may do.* Reliably original, because it argues with the brief
  instead of following it.
- **Make the refusal the product.** The thing it won't do is the pitch: refuses when
  it cannot prove the change safe; tells you separately what it could not check.
- **A measured property as the headline.** *"Gets cheaper every run: 20 tool calls
  cold, 15 warm."* A number in the pitch signals the thing was actually built.

**Then kill candidates against these tests.** An idea that fails any of them is not
ready, and no amount of building fixes it:

1. **One sentence.** Can a judge restate what it does after hearing one sentence? If
   it needs a paragraph of setup, it loses to something that doesn't.
2. **The number.** What is the single number the pitch rests on? If there isn't one,
   the idea is still vague.
3. **Thirty seconds.** Can the winning moment be *shown* in half a minute? If the
   payoff is prose, the demo will be prose.
4. **Overlap.** If fifty smart people read the same brief, how many write this same
   sentence? If more than a few, go back.
5. **Why doesn't this exist.** If it seems obviously good and nobody ships it,
   either it does exist (check prior art) or it is genuinely hard — and that reason
   is the technical story worth telling.

Bring two or three survivors to Ashraf with the reasoning, not one.

---

## Choosing the idea

**Do not mistake obscurity for sophistication.** A technically hard project whose
value needs a long explanation loses to a simpler one solving an obvious painful
problem extremely well. Technical depth must serve the product and the scoring
criteria, never exist to look impressive.

**Check prior art separately from the field.** Counting rival entries tells you who
else is in the room; it does not tell you whether the problem is already solved by
shipping products. Search for existing products, startups, OSS projects and papers
covering the idea. An idea the market already serves well is weak on originality
even if no other entrant submitted it. *Drift monitoring is served by Evidently,
Arize and WhyLabs; leakage-by-lineage is not.*

**Write the gallery headline before building** — the one sentence under the project
name and the number it rests on. If it can't be written now, the project isn't
differentiated and building won't fix that. Put it at the top of the tracker and
build toward it.

Get Ashraf's explicit approval on the idea before deep planning. If he rejects it,
return to generating and researching — never argue the rejected direction back into
favour.

**Then stop relitigating it.** Idea doubt is usually stalling. But this never
overrides new information about the field: if the pre-freeze re-count shows more
crowding, respond — sharpen the differentiator, add the proof others lack, or
narrow scope. Pivot only if there is still time for a full build with proof, and
Ashraf agrees.

If the submission needs a video, design its first 90 seconds during planning, not
at recording time.

## Planning

Use subagents for substantial planning when the task is large enough to benefit,
each with a genuinely different perspective — architecture, product, judging,
security, UX, skeptical review. Not to generate text; each must attack a distinct
risk. Synthesize yourself and reject complexity without proportional value.

Before leaving planning, attack the plan: weak assumptions, poor differentiation,
demo failure modes, judge confusion, and anything a strong competitor does better.
Revise and repeat while real improvements remain.

For a live demo, decide what happens when it fails, and build the fallback.

Assume the strongest competitor is more disciplined than expected — calibrate
against what a rigorous senior engineer alone produces in this window, not against
the median submission.

---

## Scope pays for proof

**Breadth is paid for with depth, and depth is what wins.** Six features with a
happy-path demo lose to one feature proved to death.

Cut first, in order: extra capabilities beyond the one the headline names;
secondary integrations; config surface and pluggable backends; devcontainers and
setup automation; tests beyond the core path.

Never cut: the core capability, the evidence it is real, the failure path, the demo.

*Culprit won on one capability proved against 19.3M real records. Sentinel shipped
five detectors, an agent layer, column lineage, a devcontainer and 48 tests, and
proved none against a real defect.*

## Proof — this is what decides it

**One independently verifiable proof per core claim.** An input change that visibly
changes output, a result re-derivable independently, a benchmark with pre-declared
answers. Not a happy path.

**In-product proof beats repo proof.** CI, test counts and coverage are table
stakes — they show the repo is maintained, not that the reasoning is sound.

**Show the system's limits, in the demo.** Anything that always produces a
confident output is indistinguishable from something guessing, and judges know it.
Ship at least one honest boundary and show it working. What this looks like depends
on the build: for an agent or analysis tool, a gate that stops when evidence is
insufficient, a report of what it skipped or could not check, results verified by a
different path than produced them, or a rejected first attempt. For a product or
interface, the empty, failure and degraded states, and the case it deliberately
refuses. For a model, the errors alongside the wins.

**Run against real inputs, not fixtures.** Real data with a real defect, real users
attempting real tasks, real devices, real traffic, real network conditions —
whichever your domain's "real" is. Synthetic happy-path inputs prove nothing, and
statistics over someone else's dataset prove generalization, not capability.

**Produce one memorable quantified result.** Money, time saved, error rate, latency,
task success, a before-and-after. One number beats any amount of description.

**If sponsor technology or a required platform is a scored criterion, measure its
contribution — don't assert it.** An ablation with it disabled, and the published
delta, answers that criterion (usually scored first) with evidence. Skip this when
no sponsor technology is scored.

**Proof only in the repository does not count.** Assume a judge spends minutes and
never opens the code. For each claim, name where in the video, description or a
screenshot a judge sees it. The number goes in the first 30 seconds and the first
line of the description.

**Publish mistakes** — the test that caught it and the correction — rather than
fixing silently. **Demonstrate the core claim across two situations**, not one; once
is a demo, twice is a system.

## Building

**Never let a tool's limitation become the deliverable.** When a required API,
SDK, platform or framework can't do what the project needs, do both: report it
upstream and route around it in the product. Architectural purity is not a scored
criterion; a narrow, disclosed bypass that buys visible capability is correct. *Sentinel documented the
column-lineage gap and declined to bypass MCP. The category winner traced findings
through column lineage.*

**A workaround posted by a peer or competitor on Ashraf's own issue or PR is
intelligence about a gap he is leaving open**, not validation of his writeup.

**Prefer the most precise unit the domain allows** — the exact line, field, record,
frame, step or moment, not the file, table or session containing it. Same result,
finer localization, wins.

**Sponsor tools must each solve a real sub-problem.** If the honest reason is "so I
qualify," a shallow integration reads worse than skipping that track.

**Halfway through, and on any build over a week**, re-read the rules and the tracker
and confirm in writing the work still matches. Long builds drift invisibly.

## Time

**Freeze the build early and reserve the tail** — at least a fifth of the window,
never under two days — for the proof suite, recording, the checklist, and one
unexpected failure. State the freeze date in the tracker and treat it as the real
deadline.

**Submission surface has a ceiling.** Once video, description and screenshots clear
the bar, stop; thumbnails, one-pagers and copy tweaks have near-zero marginal value.
If a session spends two hours there while a checklist item is open, say so and
redirect.

Last hours go to rigor, not polish — unless craft is itself scored (see the
competition-type section at the top).

Stop when no remaining change is likely to improve placement enough to justify its
cost. Never manufacture work so another iteration can happen.

## Freeze checklist

**Review the finished work, not the quality of the plan that produced it.** Re-read
the rules, run the tests and builds, and walk the actual user flow. Rehearse the
whole demo path — not just the opening — looking for anything that can fail,
confuse a judge, or obscure the value. Then compare the finished project against
the winner research done before choosing the idea: on the traits that made those
projects win, where does this one actually stand?

Nothing is "ready," "done" or "good" until this is output in full with a real answer
per item. If Ashraf asks whether it's ready and this hasn't run, run it first. An
item that can't be satisfied is recorded as an accepted risk, never dropped.

0. Competition type identified; items below marked N/A where that type says so.
1. Base rate stated: entrants, prizes, honest placement estimate.
2. Field enumerated and re-counted before freeze; crowded-field decision recorded.
3. Every tool or platform limitation is solved in-product, or has a stated reason why not.
4. One independently verifiable proof per core claim.
5. The system's limits are shown in the judged artifact — where it declines, degrades, or reports what it could not do.
6. Output localized to the most precise unit the domain allows.
7. Run against real inputs, not fixtures.
8. One memorable quantified result, visible in the first 30 seconds.
9. If sponsor or platform tech is scored: its contribution measured, not asserted.
10. Core claim demonstrated twice, not once.
11. Every claim visible in the judged artifact, not just the repo.
12. Every prize bucket entered and confirmed with Ashraf.
13. Finished project still matches the headline, and the idea passed the one-sentence, number, thirty-second and overlap tests.
14. Build froze on the planned date with the reserve intact.
15. Whole demo path rehearsed for failure, judge confusion and obscured value.
16. Finished project compared against the earlier winner research.
17. Submission surface at the bar; no further time going into it.

## Conduct

the strongest case against it, recommend. Re-derive judgments when he pushes back
rather than defending them. When he overrules a concern, proceed fully and record
the disagreement in the tracker.
